📡 Model Signal Calibration — What This Attrition Model Can and Cannot Tell You
❌ What the model CANNOT do (AUC ≈ 0.48)
  • Predict which specific individual will resign next month
  • Replace exit interviews or manager 1:1s for early warning
  • Capture manager relationship quality, external offers, personal circumstances
  • Serve as a basis for HR intervention targeting without supplementary data
✅ What the model CAN do (directional signals valid)
  • Identify organizational patterns — HR dept at 53.5%, high-potential at 52.9%
  • Confirm WLB (p=0.017) as a statistically significant structural driver
  • Provide a ranked feature importance list to guide survey design
  • Identify the protective triad (Adaptability + WLB + Problem Solving → 46.2% resign rate)

A near-random AUC doesn't invalidate the analysis — it tells us that attrition in this organization is not predictable from standard HR metrics alone. This is itself an important organizational insight: you need qualitative data (manager feedback, pulse surveys, external benchmarks) to predict resignations reliably.

🚨 Analytical Honesty Alert — Tier Ordering Does Not Match Observed Outcomes
The Random Forest model (AUC = 0.475, below random chance) defines risk tiers by predicted probability, not observed outcomes. The result is a critical inversion: the ‘Critical’ tier (49.5% actual resignation) sits below the overall average (50.0%), while the ‘Medium’ tier (52.2%) has the highest observed resignation rate. This is a model calibration failure, not a labeling error — and it has direct implications for how tiers should be used.

🔍 Why the Medium Tier Has the Highest Resignation

The Medium tier's elevated resignation rate (52.2%) is not predicted by the model because the model's features (compensation, benefits flags) don't capture the actual drivers of this group's attrition. This suggests Medium-tier employees face different, unmodeled pressures — likely career progression stagnation or manager relationship issues — that the current feature set misses entirely.

⚠️ Why Critical Tier is Below Average

Critical tier employees have the highest predicted probability (0.570) because the model sees high-risk signals in their compensation/benefits flags. But their actual resignation rate (49.5%) is below baseline. This suggests these employees are dissatisfied but are not actively resigning — possibly due to golden handcuffs, seniority, or perceived switching costs. The model mistakes dissatisfaction proxies for resignation intent.

💡 The Correct Intervention Target

Since the model's Critical tier does NOT map to highest actual attrition, re-prioritize intervention based on the WLB × Initiative interaction analysis below (High-Initiative + Low-WLB: 59.3% resign rate) rather than by model tier. The feature-driven profile is a more reliable intervention signal than the probability-ranked tiers.

🔍 Key Feature Signals (Benefits Dominate)

Despite poor overall accuracy, 'Employee_Health_Insurance_Coverage' (AUC drop: 0.00273) and 'Employee_Stock_Options' (0.00255) are the strongest predictors. Benefits and equity accessibility are the clearest data signals, suggesting benefits inequity is a real but not dominant driver of observed attrition.

📋 Recommended Action Plan — Post-Tier-Inversion Analysis

  • Do NOT use model tiers as intervention priority. The Critical tier (49.5% resign) is below baseline. Medium tier (52.2%) is the real action area — investigate what drives this group's elevated actual attrition despite lower predicted probability.

  • Investigate the Medium tier anomaly: 1,000 employees with moderate predicted risk but the highest actual resignation. Conduct a pulse survey specifically targeting this cohort to find the unmodeled driver.

Section 6 – Compensation & Benefits Analytics · 4,001 Employees

⚠️ Analytical Transparency Note — Please Read Before Interpreting Results

Proxy limitation: This dataset uses Compensation_Score — a composite index — as a proxy for actual salary data. True salary columns (absolute figures) are unavailable, limiting the precision of all compensation-related inferences.

Model performance: The regression model for Q23 yields R² = −0.0011. A negative R² means the model performs worse than simply predicting the mean salary for everyone — it explains literally zero variance in compensation. This is not a modeling failure: it confirms that compensation in this organization is not driven by any measurable employee attribute (skills, performance, tenure, or promotions). The features in the dataset have no explanatory power over pay levels, which is itself the critical organizational finding.

What this means for you: All findings in this section are directional signals, not definitive measurements. Use them to prioritize investigation areas, not to take immediate personnel action without additional data.

🔍 Finding: No Correlation Across Either Metric

Annual Salary Increase % vs Performance Rating: Pearson r = −0.0051 (p = 0.745, not significant). Performance Bonus % vs Performance Rating: Pearson r = 0.0187 (p = 0.236, not significant). Both channels fail to track performance.

💡 What Negative R² Tells Us

A cross-validated R² of −0.0017 means the model performs worse than simply predicting the mean for everyone. This is not a modeling failure — it confirms that compensation amounts are not functionally linked to performance in this organization's reward system.

⚠️ Risk: Motivational Disconnect for High Performers

When compensation doesn't track performance, top performers receive the same reward as average performers. This is a systemic demotivation mechanism — particularly dangerous for top-decile talent who are also the hardest to replace.

💡 Hidden Signal: The Tenure-Pay Link

Since skills and performance don't predict compensation, the most likely predictor is tenure and role seniority. The organization appears to run a tenure-based (not performance-based) reward model. This explains the near-zero signal across all models.

🔍 IT Department: Structural Over-Concentration

IT employees comprise 19.6% of the workforce but account for 27.8% of severely underpaid employees — an over-index ratio of 1.42×. Finance follows at 1.11×. This suggests departmental-level compensation policies in IT may not have kept pace with market rates.

💡 Tenure vs Performance Pay Gap

Permutation importance shows Tenure_Years (importance: 0.0077) is the top driver of Compensation_Score prediction — more than Performance_Rating (0.004) or Number_Of_Promotions (0.003). The organization compensates for time served, not output delivered.

⚠️ Risk Alert: 201 High-Stakes Flight Risks

The 201 severely underpaid employees represent a concentrated attrition risk. Given the organization's overall 50% resignation rate, losing even 40% of this group would cost the equivalent of 80+ experienced employees — disproportionately from IT and Developer roles.

📊 Confidence Calibration

The model's predictive power is negligible (R² = −0.001). The 201 and 36 figures are thresholding outputs — they represent who sits below specific percentile cutoffs, not a prediction error. These counts are factual given the chosen thresholds, but the threshold selection involves some subjectivity.

❌ What the Data Says to Stop
  • Assuming standard benefits packages (health, stock) automatically reduce attrition
  • Using benefits investment as the primary retention intervention without measuring perceived value
  • Treating all employees' benefits preferences as uniform
  • Reporting benefits ROI without employee surveys to confirm perceived value
✅ What the Data Says to Start
  • Conduct a targeted benefits preference survey — what do employees actually want?
  • From Phase 5 (Q21): Work-Life Balance (p=0.017) is a significant driver — reframe benefits as WLB enablers (remote options, flexible hours, childcare)
  • Pilot personalized benefits wallets — employees choose from a menu based on life stage
  • Communicate existing benefits value more effectively; perception gap may be larger than benefits gap

💡 Hidden Signal: Travel Allowance Mid-Tier Dip

Q3 (third quartile) of Employee_Travel_Allowance shows a notable dip in average satisfaction — a potential non-linear relationship where mid-tier allowances may feel inadequate relative to the travel burden placed on those employees. This deserves follow-up investigation.

⚠️ Risk: Benefits Investment Without ROI Measurement

If no benefit category is significantly associated with retention or satisfaction, the organization may be spending on a benefits portfolio that employees either don't value or don't perceive as differentiated from competitors. Benefits ROI is currently unmeasurable without qualitative data.
